A2

By finding corresponding points on both images, we can solve for a homography matrix.

Starting from the initial matrix, we can manipulate the equations to isolate the homography values in terms of the reference points and transformed points. The steps are shown below in greater detail.

$$ P' = \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ w \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} h_{11} & h_{12} & h_{13} \\ h_{21} & h_{22} & h_{23} \\ h_{31} & h_{32} & h_{33} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

$$ \begin{aligned} a &= h_{11}x + h_{12}y + h_{13} \\ b &= h_{21}x + h_{22}y + h_{23} \\ w &= h_{31}x + h_{32}y + h_{33} \end{aligned} $$

To convert back to 2D Cartesian coordinates, divide \(a\) and \(b\) by \(w\):

$$ x' = \frac{a}{w} = \frac{h_{11}x + h_{12}y + h_{13}} {h_{31}x + h_{32}y + h_{33}} $$ $$ y' = \frac{b}{w} = \frac{h_{21}x + h_{22}y + h_{23}} {h_{31}x + h_{32}y + h_{33}} $$

Through some algebraic manipulation, we can isolate x' and y' in terms of the H and the known points.


$$ x' = h_{11}x + h_{12}y + h_{13} - h_{31}x'x - h_{32}x'y - h_{33}x' $$ $$ y' = h_{21}x + h_{22}y + h_{23} - h_{31}y'x - h_{32}y'y - h_{33}y' $$
For each point: $$ \begin{bmatrix} x & y & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x'x & -x'y \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x & y & 1 & -y'x & -y'y \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} h_{11} \\ h_{12} \\ h_{13} \\ h_{21} \\ h_{22} \\ h_{23} \\ h_{31} \\ h_{32} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \end{bmatrix} $$

We have effectively changed the matrix to a format that makes the H matrix our unknown and the goal to solve for.

As we populating the matrix with more point pairs, we will add more to the number of rows and form an overdetermined system, where we can then use least squares to solve, and find H.

A3

Now, we will try to warp one image to match the other. To break it down a bit more, we will show the warped results from 2 different interpolation methods. The left warped images are from nearest neighbors, where directly round each coordinate to their closest neighbor's pixel values. The right warped picture comes from a bilinear approach, where we take the weighted average of the 4 corner pixel closest to our current location.

Consistent for all the pictures, bilinear took longer than nearest neighbor. This makes sense since nearest neighbor only requires two rounding operations, while we would need to calculate the pixel of values of all 4 surrounding pixels and then do a weighted average for the bilinear method. Since I did make my images really small, it is much harder to see the differences, but when enlarged, the bilinear approach has smoother transition lines especially at edges and corners compared to nearest neighbor. However, since the difference in quality and speed isn't huge, I decided to use nearest neighbor when calculating the images since if I use bigger images, the time may scale.

PART B

Now, we will experience with methods of auto-stitching instead of manually finding the correspondence points like in Part A.

B1

First, we will look at the Harris Corners, which is a way to quickly identify key points that we were manually looking earlier - corners. These corners are essentially points in the image where the intensity changes significantly in all directions. The Harris Corner Detector achieves this by analyzing the gradient of the image and calculating a response value for each pixel, which indicates the likelihood of that pixel being a corner.
Next, we will apply Adaptive Non-Maximal Suppression (ANMS) to filter out the Harris corners and retain only the most significant ones. This helps in reducing the number of points while keeping the most relevant ones for further processing.

B2

Taking the same image above (wheeler), we now apply a feature descriptor function. Taking the same image above (wheeler), we now apply a feature descriptor function. The descriptor function extracts unique and identifiable features from the image by analyzing small regions around each detected corner. More specifically, we 40x40 window around each corner, resizes them to 8x8, and normalizes the values to ensure consistency across features. This process helps in creating a compact representation of the image's key points, making it easier to compare and match features between images.

The image below displays 10 examples of these descriptors extracted from an image of Evans, showcasing the distinct patterns captured by the function.

B3

The image below illustrates the feature matching process. The lines connecting the two images represent the matched features, where each line corresponds to a pair of points that have been identified as similar based on their descriptors. The matching process uses the L2 norm to measure the distance between feature descriptors, and Lowe's ratio test is applied to ensure that the matches are robust by selecting only those matches where the best match is significantly better than the second-best match.

When matching, I also ensured that I am matching from both directions to ensure the correct set of mapping exist.

B4

Finally, we implement RANSAC, a method to filter out outliers among the matched features. RANSAC works by iteratively selecting a random subset of matches to compute a homography matrix (H) and then evaluating how well this matrix aligns the remaining matches. The process repeats and we want to keep the H with the highest number of inliers (matches that fit well). This ensures that the final set of matches is highly reliable, which is then used the calculate the actual H that we use for warping our images.
